### 1. Neptune's discovery in 1846

Tag: HISTORICAL_RECORD
Recommended wording:
"On 23 September 1846, Johann Gottfried Galle and Heinrich d'Arrest identified Neptune at the Berlin Observatory, approximately 55 arcminutes from the position predicted by Urbain Le Verrier using Newtonian gravitational calculations. ### 2. Halley's comet and the 1910 return
Verdict: Correct, with a chronological qualification
Tag: HISTORICAL_RECORD
Recommended wording:
"Using Newtonian celestial mechanics and calculated planetary perturbations, Philip Herbert Cowell and Andrew Claude de la Cherois Crommelin predicted Halley's comet's 1910 return. Their pre-recovery calculation placed perihelion around 17 April 1910, approximately 2.7 days earlier than the actual passage around 19–20 April. Observations after the comet's recovery in 1909 permitted a more accurate prediction."
The approximately three-day discrepancy belongs specifically to the original calculation, not to every subsequent forecast. Cowell and Crommelin revised their orbital predictions after the comet was recovered.
This is an excellent counterexample for dialogue with a Newtonian astronomer: the theory could predict the return of a comet after roughly three-quarters of a century, despite accumulated perturbations and imperfect orbital knowledge.
Sources: Cowell & Crommelin (1908), The Perturbations of Halley's Comet, 1759–1910; J. Jackson (1950), Philip Herbert Cowell, MNRAS 110, 125–128.

### 3. Mercury's anomalous perihelion advance
Verdict: Correct
Tag: HISTORICAL_RECORD
Recommended wording:
"Le Verrier identified an unexplained advance of Mercury's perihelion of approximately 38 arcseconds per century in 1859. Newcomb's later calculations revised the discrepancy to approximately 43 arcseconds per century. The anomaly represented the residual after known Newtonian planetary perturbations had been accounted for—not Mercury's total perihelion precession."
The distinction between the three angular rates is essential.
Quantity
	Approximate arcseconds/century
	
Observed precession relative to the moving equinox
	5,600″
	
Precession relative to an approximately inertial reference frame
	574–575″
	
Newtonian planetary contribution
	532″
	
Unexplained residual
	43″
	

The first two values are not competing measurements: they use different reference frames. The roughly 5,025″/century difference comes principally from the precession of Earth's equinox.
Do not write that Mercury's orbit precesses by only 43″ per century. It is the additional anomalous advance that amounts to approximately 43″.
Sources: Le Verrier (1859); Newcomb (1882); Zeleny (2008), Mercury's Perihelion Precession; MNRAS (2011), Least-action perihelion precession.

### 5. Einstein's 1911 and 1915 deflection predictions
Verdict: Correct
Tag: HISTORICAL_RECORD
Recommended wording:
"In 1911, Einstein predicted that light grazing the Sun would be deflected by approximately 0.83 arcseconds. After developing his general theory of relativity in 1915, he obtained approximately twice that deflection, conventionally quoted as 1.75 arcseconds at the solar limb."
The distinction is both numerical and theoretical: Einstein's 1911 calculation preceded his completed general-relativistic treatment. His later calculation incorporated the contribution absent from the earlier prediction.
For 1919 material, use the values reported by the expedition authors:
- Zero deflection: 0″.
- Newtonian or half deflection: 0.87″.
- General relativity: 1.75″.

Sources: Einstein (1911), Über den Einfluß der Schwerkraft auf die Ausbreitung des Lichtes; Dyson, Eddington & Davidson (1920), pp. 291–292.

### 6. Soldner and the Newtonian half-deflection
Verdict: Correct, with attribution qualifications
Tag: HISTORICAL_RECORD
Recommended wording:
"Johann Georg von Soldner investigated the gravitational deflection of light using Newtonian mechanics in a paper dated 1801 and published in the astronomical yearbook for 1804. His calculation is commonly associated with a solar-limb deflection of approximately 0.84 arcseconds. Using the astronomical constants employed in 1919, the corresponding Newtonian corpuscular prediction was approximately 0.87 arcseconds. Newton himself did not publish this numerical solar-limb calculation."
The distinction between the paper's composition date and the yearbook title is important.
There is also a historiographical precaution: Soldner's calculation was not prominently credited in the original 1919 expedition paper. Dyson, Eddington and Davidson described the 0.87″ alternative through Einstein's 1911 discussion.
A Cambridge astronomer in 1917 could reasonably know the half-deflection calculation without necessarily knowing Soldner's historical priority.
Sources: Soldner (1801; yearbook for 1804), Ueber die Ablenkung eines Lichtstrals von seiner geradlinigen Bewegung; Dyson et al. (1920), pp. 291–292.

### 7. Einstein's Mercury paper of 18 November 1915
Verdict: Correct
Tag: HISTORICAL_RECORD
Recommended wording:
"On 18 November 1915, Einstein presented a paper explaining Mercury's anomalous perihelion advance through his developing general theory of relativity. His calculation yielded approximately 43 arcseconds per century, in agreement with the unexplained astronomical residual."
The precise historical qualification is that Einstein presented this calculation before his final formulation of the field equations on 25 November 1915.
His Mercury result was therefore an important success during the final development of general relativity, not an after-the-fact application to a theory completed months earlier.
Source: Einstein (1915), Erklärung der Perihelbewegung des Merkur aus der allgemeinen Relativitätstheorie, presented 18 November 1915, Sitzungsberichte, pp. 831–839.

### 8. The three 1919 eclipse results and their errors
Verdict: Needs correction and qualification
Tag: DISPUTED for the treatment of the astrographic observations; HISTORICAL_RECORD for the numerical results and stated error convention.
Recommended wording:
"The 1919 eclipse expeditions yielded a solar-limb deflection of 1.98″ ± 0.12″ from Sobral's 4-inch instrument and 1.61″ ± 0.30″ from Príncipe. Both quoted uncertainties were described as probable errors. The Sobral astrographic instrument yielded a discordant result of approximately 0.93″ under one reduction, but its observations were assigned little weight because of poor image quality and uncertainty about changes in focus and photographic scale."
Here is the source-faithful summary:
Measurement
	1920 result
	Treatment
	
Sobral, 4-inch lens
	1.98″ ± 0.12″
	Principal result; probable error
	
Príncipe, astrographic lens
	1.61″ ± 0.30″
	Secondary result; probable error
	
Sobral, astrographic lens
	0.93″
	Discounted because of systematic uncertainty
	
Sobral, alternate astrographic reduction
	1.52″
	Alternative treatment of photographic scale
	

What does "probable error" mean?
The original paper explicitly uses probable error. It does not label these values as modern one-standard-deviation uncertainties.
Under a Gaussian approximation, a probable error is approximately 0.6745 times the standard deviation of the estimate. Therefore, a rough statistical conversion gives:
- Sobral 4-inch: 0.12″ probable error corresponds to approximately 0.18″ at one standard deviation.
- Príncipe: 0.30″ probable error corresponds to approximately 0.44–0.45″ at one standard deviation.

These conversions should not be mistaken for a modern comprehensive uncertainty analysis, since systematic effects and assumptions about plate scale were central to the experiment.
Was setting aside the Sobral astrographic data justified?
The decision remains a topic of historical discussion, but the claim that Eddington personally discarded the observations to manufacture agreement with Einstein is not supported by the surviving reduction records. Dyson supervised the Greenwich reductions.
A 1979 remeasurement obtained 1.90″ ± 0.11″ for the Sobral 4-inch plates and 1.55″ ± 0.34″ for the astrographic plates, lending support to the original team's concern that the 0.93″ value was strongly affected by the measurement and reduction difficulties.
The game's honest conflict is therefore about optical quality, scale assumptions, weighting, and uncertainty—not a proven act of fraud.
Sources: Dyson, Eddington & Davidson (1920), pp. 311–312, 327–331; D. Kennefick (2009), Testing relativity from the 1919 eclipse—a question of bias; Harvey (1979), remeasurement discussed in Kennefick.

The decisive primary-document evidence is Eddington's letter to Dyson of 3 October 1919, preserved as MS.RGO.8/150, Cambridge University Library.
Eddington acknowledged Dyson's letter and measurements and expressed relief that the 4-inch Cortie plates gave the full deflection. The correspondence also shows that Eddington had previously learned of the conflicting astrographic result.
The exact day on which Dyson's preceding letter arrived is not established by that surviving reply.

### 11. Eddington's preliminary Príncipe measurement and telegram
Verdict: Correct, with a timing qualification
Tag: HISTORICAL_RECORD
Recommended wording:
"Following the eclipse of 29 May 1919, Eddington developed and measured photographs on Príncipe. During the first days of June, preliminary examination of one usable plate suggested agreement with Einstein's prediction, with some support from a second. This was not yet the final determination. His cautiously optimistic telegram was reported in Nature on 5 June 1919 as 'Through cloud, hopeful.'"
The strongest evidence is Eddington's letter to his mother, written aboard the S.S. Zaire on 21 June, with a later postscript dated 2 July.
In that letter he explained that the eclipse photographs had been developed two per night over six nights, and that he had spent the days measuring them. He also acknowledged that the cloudy weather forced him to alter his intended reduction procedure and prevented a confident preliminary announcement.
Thus the letter verifies the general early-June timing, but does not establish the exact calendar day on which the decisive preliminary plate was measured.
The telegram wording is securely attested by Nature on 5 June. That publication date is not necessarily the date the telegram was transmitted.
For a game scene, treat the telegram as an expression of hope, not as proof that the eclipse had already vindicated Einstein.
Sources: A. S. Eddington to Sarah Ann Eddington, 21 June and 2 July 1919, Trinity College Cambridge Archives, EDDN/A/4/9; Nature (5 June 1919), The Solar Eclipse.

### 12. Was Newtonian caution reasonable in Cambridge in 1917?
Verdict: Correct
Tag: HISTORICAL_RECORD
Recommended wording:
"In 1917, a careful astronomer could reasonably continue to regard Newtonian gravitation as extraordinarily successful while admitting that Mercury's perihelion advance remained unexplained. Besides a hypothetical intra-Mercurial planet, proposed explanations included the gravitational attraction of matter associated with the zodiacal light and a minute departure from the exact inverse-square force law."
Two concrete historical examples are particularly suitable for dialogue.
Seeliger's zodiacal-matter hypothesis. Hugo von Seeliger investigated whether matter responsible for zodiacal light, distributed near the Sun, could gravitationally perturb Mercury. His explanation was discussed in contemporary astronomical literature, including the 1911 Encyclopaedia Britannica. It was a proposed mechanism, not an observationally established solution.
Hall's modified force law. In 1894, Asaph Hall considered a gravitational force falling approximately as r−2.00000016r^{-2.00000016}r−2.00000016, rather than precisely r−2r^{-2}r−2. This tiny change could reproduce Mercury's anomalous advance mathematically, though its consequences for other planetary motions required scrutiny.
The larger historical point is important: Mercury's anomaly was not evidence that Newtonian mechanics had failed across astronomy. Newtonian theory continued to explain a vast range of orbital phenomena with remarkable accuracy. Rejecting it wholesale in 1917 would not have been an obvious or compulsory scientific step.
A historically reasonable Newtonian character might therefore argue that a residual 43″ per century calls for further investigation, not immediate abandonment of an otherwise successful theory.
Sources: Asaph Hall (1894), discussed in Nature 51, 183; Hugo von Seeliger (1906), Das Zodiakallicht und die empirischen Glieder in der Bewegung der innern Planeten; Encyclopaedia Britannica (1911), "Zodiacal Light."

## Final recommendation for the game's historical framing
The strongest historical structure is a genuine intellectual disagreement, not a simple contest between a stubborn Newtonian and an enlightened relativist.
In Cambridge, 1917, let Newtonian astronomy appear powerful and empirically successful. Neptune, Halley's comet and the precise calculations of planetary motion provide real reasons for confidence. Mercury remains an acknowledged unresolved exception. Einstein's new theory offers an extraordinary explanation, but its experimental predictions still require testing.
In Cambridge, September–October 1919, show how observational evidence becomes a scientific conclusion. The original photographs, comparison plates, optical difficulties, reduction sheets, correspondence and differing estimates all matter. The 3 October letter provides a particularly well-grounded narrative anchor.
After 6 November 1919, characters can discuss the formally announced results, but should still distinguish the original investigators' confidence from the stronger body of evidence accumulated in later decades.
The project's existing distinction between historical records, fictional assistants, disputed evidence and reconstructed interactions is sound. For production, retain the exact citations behind numerical displays and label the proposed October correspondence scene as reconstruction rather than documented verbatim history.
