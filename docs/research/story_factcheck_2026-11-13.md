## 1. Dyson to Cottingham: "Eddington will go mad and you will have to come home alone"
Verdict: TRUE for the wording; PARTLY for where and when.
- Original teller: Eddington himself, A. S. Eddington, "Forty Years of Astronomy", in J. Needham and W. Pagel (eds.), Background to Modern Science (CUP, 1938; lectures given 1936), essay pp. 117-142. Lemos cites it as p. 117 (1940 printing); oracle puts the anecdote near pp. 141-142. I could not open the 1938 page. Repeated in Chandrasekhar, "Verifying the theory of relativity", Notes Rec. R. Soc. 30 (1976) 249-260, p. 250 (and in his 1983 book), which is where most people read it.
- Wording (as block-quoted by Kennefick 2007 from Chandrasekhar 1976 p. 250, i.e. Eddington's text): "I remember Dyson explaining all of this to my companion Cottingham, who gathered the main idea that the bigger the result, the more exciting it would be. 'What will it mean if we get double the deflection?' 'Then,' said Dyson, 'Eddington will go mad, and you will have to come home alone.'"
- Careful: Lemos 2019 paraphrases it as "double the Einstein deflection", "Then Eddington will go mad, and you will have to come home alone!" and adds "on the day previous to the departure, in Greenwich". That location/timing is NOT in the Eddington sentence above. Treat Greenwich/the night before sailing (8 March 1919) as reconstruction. Eddington gives no place and no date.
- Context: "three possibilities" = no deflection (0), half (Newtonian, 0.87"), full (Einstein, 1.75"). Cottingham is the one who asks. Kennefick uses the story in the bias debate; it is Eddington's retrospective, written 19 years later, not a 1919 record.
- Safe phrasing: "Eddington remembered it later: Dyson was explaining the three outcomes to Cottingham, who asked what it would mean to get double the deflection. 'Then Eddington will go mad,' said Dyson, 'and you will have to come home alone.'" Tag it as Eddington's own later recollection; place the scene "before sailing" without naming a room.

## 2. Eddington to Cottingham: "you will not have to go home alone"
Verdict: UNVERIFIED (wording, day, and where recorded). Event itself is plausible but the line is not in any primary text I could open.
- Who repeats it: Lemos 2019 (p. 36 area) says Eddington "told Cottingham that he would not have to go alone" and cites Eddington 1938 [17]. Oracle-only: the standard citation is A. Vibert Douglas, Life of Arthur Stanley Eddington (Nelson, 1956), p. 40, in the form "Cottingham, you won't have to go home alone." Paul Johnson, Modern Times, places it "on the evening of 3 June" (popular history, no primary cited). A Princeton centenary piece reportedly says 30 May. I did not open Douglas or Johnson.
- What IS primary (Trinity, 21 June 1919, EDDN/A/4/9): plates were developed "2 each night for 6 nights after the eclipse, and I spent the whole day measuring"; "the one good plate that I measured gave a result agreeing with Einstein and I think I have got a little confirmation from a second plate." So the decisive measurement is early June (6th night after 29 May is about 3-4 June). No exact day is given. The "June 3" notebook entry that Lemos/Douglas quote is word-for-word close to this letter, so it is probably the same text, not independent proof of the date.
- Contemporary public record: Nature, 5 June 1919, pp. 265-266, "Through cloud, hopeful" (so the first measurement was in hand by about 4 June at the latest for that cable, or the cable preceded it; sending day unknown).
- Do NOT put a date on the line. Safe phrasing: "In the first days of June, after measuring the one good plate, he is said to have told Cottingham he would not have to go home alone." Tag RECONSTRUCTION, or DISPUTED if shown as a quotation. In dialogue, keep Eddington cautious (the letter itself says "I have not been able to make any preliminary announcement").

## 3. "Too busy changing plates"
Verdict: TRUE. Primary, exact.
- Source: Eddington to his mother, aboard S.S. "Zaire", 21 June 1919 (+ postscript Lisbon, 2 July), Trinity College Library EDDN/A/4/9. URL: https://archives.trin.cam.ac.uk/index.php/letter-from-a-s-eddington-to-sarah-ann-eddington-19
- Wording: "We had to carry out our programme of photographs in faith. I did not see the eclipse, being too busy changing plates, except for one glance to make sure it had begun, and another half-way through to see how much cloud there was. We took 16 photographs (of which 4 are not yet developed)."
- Not Space, Time and Gravitation, and not a diary: it is the letter. The book (CUP 1920, ch. 5, p. 114) has the neighbouring passage: "One observer was kept occupied changing the plates in rapid succession, whilst the other gave the exposures of the required length with a screen held in front of the object-glass."
- Division of labour, 1920 report p. 314 (para 29): "Mr. Cottingham gave the exposures and attended to the driving mechanism, and Prof. Eddington changed the dark slides."
- Tag HISTORICAL_RECORD. Phrasing: "He wrote home that he never really saw the eclipse: too busy changing plates, apart from a glance to see it had begun and another halfway through to check the cloud."

## 4. Weather, 29 May 1919
Verdict: TRUE, with a small source conflict.
- Report (Phil. Trans. A 220, p. 314, para 29): "The days preceding the eclipse were very cloudy. On the morning of May 29 there was a very heavy thunderstorm from about 10 a.m. to 11.30 a.m.-a remarkable occurrence at that time of year. The sun then appeared for a few minutes, but the clouds gathered again. About half-an-hour before totality the crescent sun was glimpsed occasionally, and by 1.55 it could be seen continuously through drifting cloud. The calculated time of totality was from 2h. 13m. 5s. to 2h. 18m. 7s. G.M.T." Also: "the cloud must have thinned considerably during the last third of totality, and some star images were shown on the later plates." And: "A few minutes after totality the sun was in a perfectly clear sky, but the clearance did not last long."
- Letter 21 June 1919 (same source as claim 3): storm "came on" as the visitors arrived, "the heaviest we have seen"; "The rain stopped about no[o]n (the eclipse was at 2.15)"; "About 1.30 when the partial phase was well advanced, we began to get glimpses of the sun, at 1.55 we could see the crescent (through cloud) almost continuously"; "Ten minutes after the eclipse the sky was beautifully clear, but it soon clouded again."
- Conflict: report says storm ended 11:30; letter says rain stopped "about noon". Use "storm in the late morning, over by noon" or "from about ten until half past eleven (the official report)". The time-zone convention behind "1.55" is not stated; the letter calls the eclipse "2.15". Avoid claiming local time.
- Also documented: the dry "gravana" wind set in about 10 May and "no rain fell except on the morning of the eclipse" (report p. 313). Guests Carneiro, the Curador, the Judge, Mr Wright and three doctors had come over that morning (letter).
- Plates: 16 exposures; first 10 "practically no stars", last 6 "a few images" (letter). Report credits only two plates with usable stars (consistent with PROVENANCE).

## 5. Roça Sundy, Jeronymo Carneiro, local labour
Verdict: TRUE for station and host. Labour: PARTLY (documented in outline; no names, numbers of builders unknown).
- Host: report p. 313: "we fixed on Roça Sundy, the headquarters of Sr. Carneiro's chief plantation. We were Sr. Carneiro's guests during our whole visit, and used freely his ample resources of labour and material at Sundy. We learnt later that he had postponed a visit to Europe in order to entertain us." Acknowledgements p. 333: "special thanks are due to Sr. Jeronymo Carneiro, who most hospitably entertained the observers..." He was met on arrival as "President of the Association of Planters" (p. 312).
- Eddington's letter 29 Apr-2 May (Trinity, ...-17): "Mr. Carneiro is our host. He is rather a young man, and owns the largest private plantation. He has only been out here two years, but his family have had the plantation a long while. In Lisbon he was a well-known bull-fighter." (Lemos 2019: aged 26 in 1919, inherited the land.) He sailed home on the Zaire with them, "for three months" (letter -19). Owner, not just administrator. The resident manager at Sundy was "Sr. Atalaya" (report; Eddington's letter spells "Atalia"): "his help and attention were invaluable"; the party lived with him five weeks. Mr Wright and Mr Lewis of the cable station (Sierra Leoneans, British subjects) interpreted. Gragera managed the Sociedade de Agricultura Colonial estates.
- Labour, primary: letter -17: "About 600 native labourers are at work on the plantation and they have carpenters and mechanics at work so it is easy to get any small things required." "We arranged to have a small pier built for the coelostat to stand on" (report: "a stone pier built for the purpose"). The party itself says: "We spent Tuesday & Wednesday erecting the two huts" (28-29 April, letter), so the huts were put up by the observers (helpers unnamed). Report p. 313 para 28: baggage went by tram "with a break of about a kilometre, where it had to be transported through the wood by native carriers." Letter -19: "a black man went with me" to watch for sharks when he bathed; Atalaya received "a succession of natives" each evening who came to see him.
- Context a writer must not soften (not in the Eddington texts beyond one line): letter -17 lists "The Curador, who is responsible for the imported labour". Principe's cocoa estates ran on contract labour (serviçais) brought from Portuguese Africa; the labour regime is the subject of Simões, Soares and Carolino, "The British and Brazilian Expeditions and the 1919 Total Solar Eclipse: Regimes of Labour and Degrees of Invisibility", BJHS (2025), DOI 10.1017/S0007087425000305 [oracle-only; not read]. 
- Safe phrasing: "Local workers on Carneiro's plantation carried the baggage through the forest and built the stone pier; Eddington's report thanks Carneiro and his manager but names none of the workers." Tag HISTORICAL_RECORD for the pier, carriers, host; RECONSTRUCTION for any named worker, any dialogue, or a worker building the huts. Any named worker character is FICTION.

## 6. Cottingham of Thrapston
Verdict: TRUE.
- E. T. Cottingham obituary, MNRAS 101 (1941) 131 (ADS 1941MNRAS.101..131.; written by a fellow Principe observer, i.e. Eddington): "Edwin Turner Cottingham was born at Ringstead, near Thrapston, Northants, on 1869 April 9... apprenticed to a tailor... started to work for a clockmaker Mr. Allen in Thrapston... on Mr. Allen's retirement took over the business. The first clock which he made was the Thrapston church clock." Later made Riefler-type escapement clocks in airtight cases (one each to Greenwich and Mauritius), Admiralty chronometer work in WWI, and for "more than thirty years" was "an honorary keeper of the clocks" at Cambridge Observatory.
- Age: Eddington to Winifred, 5 May 1919: "He is just 50" (really 50 on 9 April 1919).
- Shop in Thrapston: Lemos 2019 p. 9 (photo "around 1910"). Born in Ringstead, business in Thrapston; his home address is not in what I read.
- "Instrument maker": Lemos calls him "a gifted mechanical and electric technician". Use "clock and chronometer maker who looked after the Observatory's clocks". Tag HISTORICAL_RECORD (matches PROVENANCE row, Kennefick 2007).

## 7. Mail between England and Príncipe, 1919
Verdict: PARTLY. Two real transit times, a documented route, nothing like an average.
- Letter of 14 March (from mother) reached Eddington "on arrival at Principe" (23 April): about 40 days (letter -17).
- Letter dated "about March 28" reached him "about May 11" (letter -19): about 44 days. Between 11 May and 21 June "there have been no mails from Europe".
- 5 May (to Winifred): "I have only got Mother's letter of March 14 as yet". 29 Apr: "We expect the next mail about May 7." So roughly one boat a month, 6 weeks door to door. Safe: "six weeks was normal; three months of silence was not unusual".
- Route: Liverpool-Lisbon (Anselm) -> Funchal -> S. Vicente/Praia (Cape Verde) -> Principe on the Portuguese "Portugal" (Companhia Nacional de Navegação). Letters were posted at S. Vicente on the way (letter -15: "I shall be able to post a letter there"). Return on "Zaire", Principe to Praia 8 1/4 days, Lisbon by ~30 June; Lisbon postscript 2 July says Liverpool "about July 15 by R.M.S.P. Line".
- 1919 disruption: "a dispute between the company and the government about passage rates, and no boats have left Lisbon for a long while"; no boat from Principe until about 1 August (letter -19; report: "a strike of the steamship company").
- Fast channel: the cable station on Principe (Wright, Lewis) and a wireless message sent from the Portugal before landing (letter -17). Eddington's cable reached Dyson in time for Nature, 5 June 1919, pp. 265-266 ("Through cloud, hopeful"); Dyson cabled back that the Brazil party had succeeded (letter -19). Date and relay route of the cable remain UNVERIFIED (as PROVENANCE already says).
- Phrasing: "A letter from Cambridge took about six weeks; a cable took a day or two." Tag HISTORICAL_RECORD for the two documented letters; RECONSTRUCTION for anything about a specific mail day.

## 8. Dates: left 12 June, Liverpool 14 July
Verdict: TRUE. Report p. 314: "We left Principe on June 12, and after transhipping at Lisbon, reached Liverpool on July 14." Letter -19 confirms 12 June sailing on the Zaire (and "I suppose I shall be back about July 10", later "about July 15"). Party outbound: Liverpool 8 March (Anselm), Madeira until 9 April, Principe 23 April (report para 26). HISTORICAL_RECORD.

## 9. Stanhope trinkets (only the scene-relevant checks; none of this involves Eddington)
a) Made and sold in England/France c.1900-1905? Verdict: PARTLY/plausible, no dated 1902-05 English retail source found.
- Origin: René Dagron, Paris, patent 1859/1860; factory at Gex made lenses for the trade; Dagron's own range included pens, pencils, letter openers, penholders, needle cases, watch keys (Collectors Weekly, "Secrets of the World's Tiniest Photographs"). Factory run by Dagron's firm to 1897, then (per same article) another family "from 1905 until 1972" (Wikipedia: Eugène Reymond). Even US lenses were mostly ordered from Gex. So a 1902-05 Stanhope pencil or charm in England is not an anachronism; lens supply was French, mounted goods made anywhere.
- Objects: National Trust (Fox Talbot Museum) bone pencil/pen holders with microphotographs, catalogued "1860-1900" (object 1521195, Eiffel Tower; 1521191 Hayling Island; 1521194 Crystal Palace, marked "Manufactured in France") - nationaltrustcollections.org.uk/object/1521195. Science Museum Y1997.64.2, "Stanhope viewer optical toy, a souvenir of the Manchester Ship Canal" (undated; the canal opened 1894). Dutch Stanhopes for Queen Wilhelmina 1908 and 1909 [oracle-only].
- Not found: an English maker, price or advertisement for a pencil cap or charm in 1902-05. Say "a French-made lens in an English-made trinket" and avoid brand names.
b) Lens-less chip under a 10-15x loupe? Verdict: UNVERIFIED by any source; my physics reasoning only.
- Facts: Dagron's images were about 2 x 2 mm (450 per plate); Wikipedia: the sectioned lens "could magnify the microphotograph three hundred times" (and "Stanhope enables viewing without a microscope"). The lens, not the chip, supplies the power. I found no source that says what a bare chip shows under a loupe.
- Reasoning (flag as such): the eye resolves roughly 0.1 mm at reading distance; a 10-15x loupe lets it separate features of about 7-10 micrometres on the chip. Fine collodion/gelatin film can hold that. So a simple, high-contrast, bold picture (a few thick lines, a silhouette, a big letter) is plausibly recognisable as a shape under 10-15x; portrait detail, pencil shading and writing need roughly 50-100x. A child's crayon scrawl reduced to ~2 mm would show as a small figure only if it has big, bold strokes. Oracle's independent estimate agrees. Tag the viewing as RECONSTRUCTION; do not state a magnification as fact.
c) Could a private person commission a custom microphotograph of a child's drawing c.1902-05? Verdict: PARTLY - precedent for customer-supplied images, nothing for 1902-05.
- Documented: by end of 1861 Dagron let customers mail in a daguerreotype or carte-de-visite and get a Stanhope of it (Collectors Weekly); Dancer (Manchester, 1853) made family-portrait microphotographs, one ring owned by Queen Victoria (Public Domain Review, "Dancing Naked on the Head of a Pin"). Oracle cites an 1874 Edmund Wheeler catalogue ad (18 shillings for twelve, from a customer's photograph or negative; via Brian Stevenson, microscopist.net) [oracle-only, could not open].
- Not documented: any 1902-05 service, price or turnaround. Safe in-game: a local photographer or an instrument-maker sends the drawing away to be photographed and reduced, taking some weeks; keep the maker unnamed. Tag FICTION for the transaction, HISTORICAL_RECORD only for "Stanhope trinkets existed".

## 10. 1918 tribunal, Dyson, and the eclipse
Verdict: PARTLY. The hearings and Dyson's letter are documented; the popular story that Dyson "tied the exemption to the expedition" is an overstatement; Chandrasekhar's telling is DISPUTED.
- Best source: Graham Dolan, Royal Observatory Greenwich, "Arthur Stanley Eddington", royalobservatorygreenwich.org/articles.php?article=1289, which transcribes Cambridge Daily News reports (British Newspaper Archive) and JPEC minutes (tribunal papers were destroyed after the war; only press reports survive).
- Chronology (all Cambridge): 10 May 1916 University gets conditional exemption on national-importance grounds (Newall); his own conscientious claim shelved. Jan 1918 cut to three months. 15 May 1918 Borough Tribunal, three months more; National Service Representative appeals. 14 June 1918 (Fri), Appeal Tribunal, County Hall: Eddington says "I am conscientious objector"; Chairman: "That question is not before us"; exemption refused, "not [to] be called until August 1st". The same day the JPEC sub-committee (Dyson, Turner, Fowler) met in the RAS rooms and provisionally agreed one party should be "Eddington and Cottingham" (JPEC minutes via Dolan). 27 June 1918 Guildhall: his personal claim cannot be heard on a technicality; adjourned to 11 July for him to get the Minister's leave ("a very hard one-hard on Prof. Eddington"); his written statement ("My objection to war is based on religious grounds...") is printed. 11 July 1918: Ministry has granted leave; Dyson's letter and others are read; decision below.
- What Dyson wrote (CDN, Fri 12 July 1918, p. 3, headline "Astronomer C.O. Given One Year Exemption. To View an Eclipse"): praises Eddington's researches ("to be ranked as highly as the work of his predecessors at Cambridge-Darwin, Ball, and Adams") and says the Joint Permanent Eclipse Committee "received a grant of 1,000 pounds for the observation of a total eclipse of the sun in May of next year... Prof. Eddington is peculiarly qualified to make these observations, and I hope the Tribunal will give him permission to undertake this task."
- Eddington told the tribunal he had been a Friend from birth, would accept the Friends' Ambulance Unit or Red Cross, and would "volunteer as an unskilled labourer for the period of the harvest"; asked about the eclipse he said he "would go if possible to view it".
- Decision: the Chairman said they were "quite convinced that Prof. Eddington was and had been, a conscientious objector", that his astronomy was "of vast importance... to knowledge generally", and gave "12 months' exemption, on condition that he continued his present work" "more especially with reference to the eclipse" next year.
- So: documented = Dyson's supporting letter naming the eclipse; the tribunal mentioned the eclipse and gave 12 months on condition he continued his present work. Not documented = a Home Office intervention (the body named in the transcript is the Ministry of National Service), a deal that he must lead the expedition, or Dyson alone saving him from prison. Wikipedia's "in particular in preparation for the Príncipe expedition" is stronger than the transcript. The war ended 11 Nov 1918, before the exemption expired.
- DISPUTED: Chandrasekhar (1973 Bull. Atomic Sci.; 1976 Notes Rec. R. Soc. 30, 249-260; book 1983) tells a version he says came from Eddington about 1933 (jail, Dyson arranging the eclipse); Dolan: "difficult to fully reconcile the facts as told by Chandrasekhar with the available contemporary sources". Also Matthew Stanley, "An Expedition to Heal the Wounds of War", Isis 94 (2003) 57-89, and Practical Mystic (2007) - not opened; the oracle reply also says the Isis date is 2003.
- Safe phrasing: "In July 1918 a Cambridge tribunal accepted that Eddington was a conscientious objector and gave him a year's exemption, so long as he kept up his astronomy; Dyson had written that Eddington was the man to observe next year's eclipse." Tag HISTORICAL_RECORD. Anything about private talks between Dyson and Eddington, or Eddington being "ready for jail" (Kennefick: "apparently prepared" per Chandrasekhar) is RECONSTRUCTION/DISPUTED. Do not imply the exemption was a trade for the experiment.
